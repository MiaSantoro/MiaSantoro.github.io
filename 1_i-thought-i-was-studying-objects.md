# I thought I was studying objects
Looking back,I'm not sure that was ever true.

What kept drawing me in was something else:the relationships that allowed those things to hold together,persist,transform and well,sometimes fall apart.

**Everything started having more sense when I stopped asking what things were and started asking how they managed to continue existing.**

Looking back,this change seems obvious.At the time,however,it happened slowly,through materials and a lot of failed experiments.Still having a lot of failed experiments actually.My interest focused on objects and materials.Through artistic practice,I worked with residual elements,industrial materials,fragments,precarious structures and processes of accumulation.

Initially,I tended to consider these elements as separate categories,each carrying a specific meaning.Over time,however,I realised that what attracted my attention was not so much the nature of the materials themselves,but *the relationships that emerged between them.* An accumulation of objects did not interest me because of the presence of its individual components,but because of the way those components managed to maintain a temporary form of cohesion.A degraded material did not appear significant because of its condition as waste but because of its ability to continue existing despite deterioration.Likewise,apparently unstable structures seemed capable of maintaining a state of balance through a network of mutual dependencies that was not immediately visible.

Gradually,the focus of the research shifted **from the object to the system.**
This transformation generated a series of questions that cut across very different fields of inquiry.

**How does a collective entity emerge from a multitude of elements?**

**Under what conditions does a structure acquire a recognisable form of organisation?**

**How do fragile,incomplete,or damaged systems continue to exist?**

These questions do not concern the field of art alone.They also emerge in the study of ecosystems,infrastructural networks,biological colonies,technological systems,and contemporary models of artificial intelligence.Despite the profound differences between these contexts,they share a fundamental characteristic:the existence of complex behaviours that cannot be attributed to a single element but emerge from the relationships between multiple components.

In recent years,this observation has become increasingly evident.
An ecosystem continues to function while undergoing processes of degradation.A city survives through invisible infrastructures that often become noticeable only when they fail.A biological colony is capable of producing adaptive behaviours without possessing a central decision-making authority.Artificial neural networks generate texts,images,and connections that cannot be localised within a single part of the system.
What these phenomena share is not a specific material or technological nature,but **their capacity to organise themselves and persist over time.**

This research begins from that observation.

**Rather than asking what objects are,it focuses on the conditions that make their persistence possible.** In this sense,the concept of persistence assumes a central role.Persistence does not coincide with stability.A persistent system may be fragile,incomplete,inefficient,or damaged.It may lose elements,change,and transform.**Its *existence* does not depend on the absence of change,but on its capacity to continue operating through change.**

This perspective also requires a reconsideration of **how the artwork is conceived.**
Traditionally,the artwork has often been understood as a finished,autonomous and self-contained object.In this research,by contrast,it is approached as an open system.Materials,forces,processes,and relationships are not merely represented;they are placed in a condition where they can interact.The interest does not lie in the construction of stable images but in the observation of configurations that emerge,transform and-I guess sometimes-collapse.
The artwork is therefore not used to illustrate a theory.Rather,*it becomes an experimental device through which a series of questions can be tested.*

This position also entails questioning some of the traditional oppositions that have historically structured Western thought:natural and artificial,living and non-living,organism and machine.The emergence of new forms of artificial intelligence,together with the growing interest in decentralised biological systems and complex ecological processes,suggests the need for more relational and less hierarchical models of understanding.

Within this context,**the production of knowledge itself appears increasingly less as the result of an isolated subject.**

A significant aspect of this research concerns precisely its own process of development.A few of the reflections that contributed to the formulation of the questions presented here emerged through dialogue with generative artificial intelligence systems.This fact is not considered merely a technological curiosity.If anything it is an exemplary case of the very problem addressed by this research.

**Ideas no longer appear as the exclusive property of an individual**,but **as phenomena that emerge within relational configurations composed of different agents.**

The question,therefore,is not whether machines might replace human beings.Rather,it concerns **the possibility of conceiving thought itself as a distributed process.**

Building on these considerations if there is a question that runs through all of this,it is a simple one:

**How do things manage to keep going?**
