## One? I'm not buying it.
13/06/2026

I spent a lot of time wondering why some things refuse to collapse.Only later did it occur to me that I had completely ignored another problem.

**How did they become "things" in the first place?**

I do have a tendency to accidentally anthropomorphise things.A pile of broken materials starts looking like it has intentions.A group of cables begins to resemble an ecosystem.A badly balanced structure somehow develops what feels suspiciously like determination.At some point I realised that maybe the problem wasn't the objects.
Maybe it was me.

Or maybe there really is something strange about the moment *when many separate things suddenly begin to behave as if they were one.*

*When does a pile become a structure?*

*When does a collection become a system?*

**And when does a multitude become an entity?**

The more I looked,the harder these questions became to ignore.I mean,just look around.

We naturally experience the world through apparently coherent units.Bodies,forests,cities,organisms,societies,infrastructures - they all appear as complete entities with clear boundaries and identities.But if you zoom in far enough,those boundaries begin to dissolve.

A body is billions of cells.A city is people,buildings,roads,electrical grids,pipes,algorithms,traffic,conversations and an alarming amount of bureaucracy.A forest is never just trees.An ecosystem is never just organisms.

Somehow,all these scattered components manage to become something we instinctively recognise as a whole.

That's the part I can't stop thinking about.

Because none of these entities actually lives inside a single element.

They emerge.

The concept of emergence I talk about attempts to describe precisely this phenomenon:**the appearance of behaviours or properties that arise from interactions between multiple elements but cannot be found in any of them individually.**

**The whole stops being the sum of its parts.It becomes the result of their relationships.**

Ant colonies provide one of the clearest examples.An individual ant knows very little,I imagine.Yet together,colonies construct elaborate structures,optimise routes,distribute resources and adapt to changing environments without any central planner.

**No ant seems to know what is happening.And somehow,funny enough but everything keeps happening anyway.**

Bird flocks behave in much the same way.Watching them move across the sky,it almost feels as though an invisible choreographer were giving instructions.In reality,every bird simply responds to the movements of its nearest neighbours.Order appears without anyone designing it.

I find this strangely comforting.Maybe coherence doesn't always require control.

The same principle appears in places that seem far removed from biology.Artificial neural networks consist of enormous numbers of relatively simple computational units.Individually,none of them understands language,images or meaning.Yet together they produce behaviours that often feel remarkably sophisticated.

Which raises an uncomfortable question.

**Where exactly is the intelligence?**

*Inside one component?*

*Inside a hidden controller?*

*Or nowhere in particular?*

**Perhaps it exists only between things.**

Neuroscience points in a similar direction.For centuries,philosophers and scientists searched for a precise location of consciousness.Contemporary research suggests something else.**There is no single place where thought resides.** Instead,cognitive processes emerge from distributed interactions across countless neural networks.

**No neuron thinks.No neuron is conscious.**

*Yet together they somehow produce what we casually call "me."*

Some of the most fascinating examples sit precisely at the boundaries of these categories.Take *Physarum polycephalum*,the slime mold.I had my own fun studying this lovely - and occasionally rather naughty - creature in my very own room.It has no brain,no nervous system,and, as far as anyone knows,no existential crises.Yet it consistently solves spatial problems,finds efficient routes between resources,and produces networks that closely resemble human transportation infrastructures.Of course,all of this remarkable work is ultimately done for the sake of its favourite snack:oat flakes.Still.

*The impressive part isn't the solution.It's that nobody is in charge.*

Again and again,**organisation emerges without central planning.**

The more examples I encountered,the more difficult it became to think of entities as fixed objects.

**Perhaps an entity does not exist before relationships.Perhaps relationships are what temporarily produce the entity.**

*A forest is not just trees.A city is not just buildings.An organism is not just organs.And maybe an artwork is not just materials.*

In every case,we go back always to the same point:coherence appears less like an inherent property and more like an **ongoing negotiation between multiple elements.**

Looking back,I think this was the moment my own research quietly changed direction.I thought I was studying objects.Instead,I found myself studying the invisible agreements that allow separate things to briefly become one.

**And maybe the interesting part isn’t when many things become one.**
Maybe it’s why we keep insisting they were ever separate.

Because once you start looking closely,the boundaries begin to feel strangely arbitrary.

*The forest leaks into the fungus.The body leaks into the microbiome.The city leaks into its infrastructure.The machine leaks into the human.*

And suddenly the categories that once felt so obvious start looking more like convenient habits than actual descriptions of reality.

Inconvenient.
